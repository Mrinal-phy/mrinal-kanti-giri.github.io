Bits vs Qubits

Amplitudes, measurement and collapse: the two rules that everything else in quantum computing is built on.

In this chapter you will

  • describe a qubit by two amplitudes, and see why they live on a circle;
  • use the Born rule to turn amplitudes into measurement probabilities;
  • watch a measurement collapse the state, and see why one shot tells you very little;
  • discover why superposition is not the same as "we don't know".
⏱ about 25 minutes🎮 4 interactive widgets✅ 7-question quiz📚 no quantum background needed
1 · Story

A bit is a switch

Every computer you have ever used stores information in bits. A bit is like a light switch: it is either 0 or 1, and you can look at it as often as you like without changing it.

A qubit (quantum bit) also only ever shows 0 or 1 when you look. But try looking at both a few times:

A bit vs a qubit

Hi, I'm Qubi, a qubit! Did you see? The bit gave the same answer every time. I gave random answers, even though every try started from exactly the same state.

But my randomness is not chaos. Over many tries you get exactly the odds my state sets, and you can control those odds. They come from two numbers called amplitudes. Let's play with them!

2 · Play

Your first qubit: two amplitudes

A qubit's state is written

\( |\psi\rangle = \alpha\,|0\rangle + \beta\,|1\rangle \)

The symbols \(|0\rangle\) and \(|1\rangle\) ("ket zero", "ket one") are the two possible outcomes. The numbers \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) are the amplitudes. In this chapter they are ordinary real numbers (positive or negative); in Chapter 2 they become complex. The one rule is

\( \alpha^2 + \beta^2 = 1 \)

so the pair \((\alpha, \beta)\) is a point on a circle of radius 1. That makes a qubit an arrow. Drag the gold knob:

State dial

Things to notice:

  • The arrow's shadow on each axis is an amplitude: α on the |0⟩ axis, β on the |1⟩ axis.
  • Amplitudes can be negative (try the |−⟩ preset). The probabilities α² and β² never are.
  • |+⟩ and |−⟩ have exactly the same probability bars. Remember this; it matters in section 4.
Show me the math

Kets are column vectors in a two-dimensional space:

$$ |0\rangle = \begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix},\qquad |1\rangle = \begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix},\qquad |\psi\rangle = \begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\ \beta\end{pmatrix}. $$

In general \(\alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{C}\) and normalisation reads \(|\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = \langle\psi|\psi\rangle = 1\). With real amplitudes we can write \(\alpha = \cos\varphi,\ \beta = \sin\varphi\): that angle \(\varphi\) is exactly what the dial shows.

Two arrows pointing in opposite directions, \(|\psi\rangle\) and \(-|\psi\rangle\), describe the same physical state: an overall ("global") sign or phase can never be measured. That is why the full picture of a qubit is not this circle but the Bloch sphere (Chapter 2), where the angle is doubled: \(\theta_{\text{Bloch}} = 2\varphi\).

3 · Play

Measurement: the Born rule

Here's the twist: you can never see α and β directly. When you measure a qubit you get a single bit, and which one is random:

\( P(0) = |\alpha|^2, \qquad P(1) = |\beta|^2 \)

This is the Born rule. And measuring changes the qubit: right after you see 1, the state is \(|1\rangle\). This is called collapse. The lab below uses the state you set on the dial.

Measurement lab

Try this. Pick |+⟩ on the dial and press Measure once a few times: completely random. Now press × 1000: the bars settle onto the dashed Born-rule lines.

Then switch to keep measuring the same qubit. After the first result, every later shot agrees with it. That's collapse! One shot tells you almost nothing about α and β. You need many fresh copies.

Show me the math

The probability of outcome \(k\) is the squared overlap with \(|k\rangle\), and the state afterwards is \(|k\rangle\):

$$ P(k) = |\langle k|\psi\rangle|^2, \qquad |\psi\rangle \;\xrightarrow{\ \text{outcome } k\ }\; |k\rangle . $$

How precise is a measured probability? Say you measure \(N\) fresh copies and get 1 in \(N_1\) of them. Your estimate of \(P(1)\) is the fraction \(\hat p = N_1/N\). How far can it wander from the true \(p\)?

Each shot is a coin flip that lands on 1 with probability \(p\). For \(N\) independent flips, the count \(N_1\) has average \(Np\) and spread (standard deviation) \(\sqrt{N\,p\,(1-p)}\). That's the standard result for a binomial distribution. Dividing by \(N\) turns the count into a fraction:

$$ \Delta p \;=\; \frac{\sqrt{N\,p\,(1-p)}}{N} \;=\; \sqrt{\frac{p\,(1-p)}{N}} . $$

Worked example for \(|+\rangle\), where \(p = 0.5\):

\(p(1-p) = 0.5 \times 0.5 = 0.25\).

\(N = 100\): \(\;0.25/100 = 0.0025\), and \(\sqrt{0.0025} = 0.05\), so about ±5%.

\(N = 1000\): \(\;0.25/1000 = 0.00025\), and \(\sqrt{0.00025} \approx 0.016\), so about ±1.6%.

Rule of thumb: four times more shots halves the error, because of the square root. That's why real quantum experiments repeat every circuit thousands of times. The measurement lab above shows this ± value live as you measure.

4 · Key insight

Superposition is not "we don't know"

Here is a fair question. When \(|+\rangle\) gives 0 half the time and 1 half the time, maybe the qubit was really 0 or 1 all along, and we just didn't know which? Like a coin hidden under a cup.

Let's test that idea with an experiment. We have three boxes:

  • \(|+\rangle\) and \(|-\rangle\), from the state dial;
  • a secret coin: each time, we secretly prepare either \(|0\rangle\) or \(|1\rangle\) at random. This is exactly the "we just don't know" idea.

Step 1. Measure all three. Step 2. Do one extra thing before measuring: apply the Hadamard gate H, a standard quantum operation (you'll meet it properly in Chapter 3). Compare the results.

The secret-coin test

Step 1: all three look the same, 50/50. Step 2: after H, \(|+\rangle\) gives 0 every time, \(|-\rangle\) gives 1 every time, but the secret coin is still 50/50.

So \(|+\rangle\) is not a secret coin. Something else is going on.

What is going on? Follow the amplitudes

H takes each part of the state and splits it into a bit of \(|0\rangle\) and a bit of \(|1\rangle\). For \(|+\rangle\), both parts send an amplitude of \(+\tfrac12\) to the outcome 0, and these add up to 1. To the outcome 1, one part sends \(+\tfrac12\) and the other sends \(-\tfrac12\), and these cancel to 0. The widget shows this as coloured blocks after Step 2.

It works like waves in water: two crests make a bigger wave, but a crest meeting a trough leaves calm water. This is interference.

The secret coin can't do this. It is either 0 or 1, never both, so there is nothing to add up or cancel: each possibility separately gives 50/50.

The big lesson. "We don't know" means probabilities, which can only add. Superposition means amplitudes, which can be negative, so they can cancel. That cancelling is the engine of every quantum algorithm.

Show me the math

The Hadamard gate acts on the basis states as

$$ H|0\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\big(|0\rangle + |1\rangle\big), \qquad H|1\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\big(|0\rangle - |1\rangle\big). $$

Apply it to \(|+\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle + |1\rangle)\), one part at a time:

$$ H|+\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}H|0\rangle + \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}H|1\rangle = \big(\tfrac12 + \tfrac12\big)|0\rangle + \big(\tfrac12 - \tfrac12\big)|1\rangle = |0\rangle . $$

Each \(\tfrac12\) is a product \(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\cdot\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\): the block values in the widget. In the same way \(H|-\rangle = |1\rangle\).

The secret coin is a mixture: \(|0\rangle\) with probability ½ and \(|1\rangle\) with probability ½. After H, each case gives 0 with probability ½, so the total is \(\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12 + \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12 = \tfrac12\). Probabilities are never negative, so they can't cancel. (In density-matrix language, the mixture is \(\rho = I/2\) and \(H\rho H^\dagger = I/2\).)

6 · Why it matters

God, dice and a footnote

The story

In June 1926, Max Born was trying to understand what Schrödinger's new "wave function" actually meant. In his paper on collisions he first wrote that the wave function itself gave the probability. Then, while correcting the proofs, he added a footnote: the probability is proportional to the square. That footnote is the Born rule you just used, and it won him the Nobel Prize in 1954.

Not everyone was happy. A few months later Albert Einstein wrote to Born:

"Quantum mechanics is certainly imposing. But an inner voice tells me that it is not yet the real thing. The theory says a lot, but does not really bring us any closer to the secret of the 'old one'. I, at any rate, am convinced that He is not playing at dice." Albert Einstein to Max Born, 4 December 1926 (translated by Irene Born)

Every experiment since has sided with Born: the randomness you saw in the measurement lab is not a gap in our knowledge. It is how nature works.

  1. 1926Born's footnote: probability = amplitude squared.
  2. 1935Schrödinger's cat: superposition taken to the absurd (and entanglement is born, Chapter 5).
  3. 1982Richard Feynman: to simulate nature, build a computer out of quantum parts.
  4. 1994Peter Shor: interference of amplitudes can factor large numbers exponentially faster.
  5. 2019A 53-qubit superconducting chip performs a sampling task beyond the comfortable reach of classical supercomputers.

💡 Did you know?

🧮

One qubit needs 2 amplitudes, two qubits need 4, and every extra qubit doubles the count: \(n\) qubits need \(2^n\). Just 50 qubits need \(2^{50} \approx 10^{15}\) numbers, about 18 petabytes of memory on a classical computer.

🌌

The state of 300 qubits needs \(2^{300} \approx 10^{90}\) amplitudes. That's more than the number of atoms in the observable universe (about \(10^{80}\)).

🎲

The randomness you saw in the measurement lab is the real thing, not a computer's fake randomness. Quantum random number generators are sold as devices for cryptography and security.

🔍

The catch: measuring \(n\) qubits only ever gives you \(n\) bits. You can't read all those amplitudes out! A quantum algorithm has to make the wrong answers cancel and the right ones add up before you look, just like the secret-coin test.
